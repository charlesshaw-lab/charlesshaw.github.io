Robotic Camera Arm

Conceptual Design — Team of 4 · Fall 2025 · SolidWorks
Final crane concept on a tripod — hip rotation, boom extension, tilt, and a 3-axis DJI RS 4 Pro gimbal give 6 DOF.
Final Report (PDF) Crane Assembly Drawing (PDF) All Engineering Drawings ↓

A low-cost, long-reach robotic camera arm for wedding photographers, who shoot 10–12 hour days and can't afford $100K+ industrial rigs. The team compared three radically different concepts, selected the crane with a weighted decision matrix, and developed it into a fully modeled and costed design: a 1.2 m telescoping boom on a tripod, for $4,696.75 against an $8,000 cap.

Design Requirements
Designed for wedding photographers as a low-cost alternative to $100K+ industrial camera arms. The closest comparable, the EVO, costs $10,000 with 0.85 m reach and 5 kg payload.

Design & Iteration

Three concepts were developed in parallel to cover the design space, each iterated against the spec list before comparison.

01
Wrist-Elbow-Shoulder — iterated 1A→1C, adding a DOF each round and designing a stepper/bearing joint transmission, but never reaching 6 DOF
02
Exoskeleton — the first draft's suspended-wire system needed 8 motors; 3B swapped it for a column on 3D-printer-style tracks
03
Crane (selected) — 2A reworked into 2B: prismatic extension, only 3 motors vs. 5, and a tripod base that unscrews to pack flat
Concept Selection
Scored 1–3 per criterion against five weighted specs. Portability was the crane's known weakness, addressed by making the boom detachable from the tripod.

Final Design
SubsystemImplementation
ShoulderBoom extends on a rack and pinion driven through bevel gears by a servo motor, with a planetary gearbox and 6061-T6 aluminum shafts.
Hip rotation24 V brushed DC motor with a 100:1 planetary gearbox for 180°/s rotation, on a thrust bearing.
Tilt (control arms)Dual rack-and-pinion control arms drive the boom up and down.
GimbalDJI RS 4 Pro adds the final 3 DOF and stabilization (4.5 kg capacity).
BaseOff-the-shelf Mitchell heavy-duty tripod, rated above the ~100 lb assembly.
Analysis & Validation
Objective
Confirm the design could meet motor torque, mass, and cost targets before a physical build.
Method
SolidWorks mass properties analysis; hand-calculated motor torque at full extension.
Result
Mass 45.9 kg (101.2 lb) — within tripod capacity; shoulder torque requirement 2.67 N·m; total cost $4,696.75 — under the $8,000 cap.

Integration & Outcome

Shoulder (rack-and-pinion + bevel gears), hip rotation (24V DC motor, 100:1 planetary), control arm, and DJI RS 4 Pro gimbal integrated onto a Mitchell Heavy-Duty tripod.

Conceptual design complete; not yet fabricated. Next: emergency stop + thermal management, then detailed mechanical analysis, tolerancing, and prototype testing with photographers.

Team of 6 · Client: Elaya Inc. · University of Toronto

FlexWrap — NMES Leg Brace

Medical Device Design & Testing · Winter 2025
Problem Context

Roughly 230,800 adults are admitted to Canadian ICUs each year, and up to 50% of them go on to develop ICU-Acquired Weakness (ICUAW) — muscle wasting brought on by prolonged immobility and critical illness. Neuromuscular Electrical Stimulation (NMES) is an established way to slow that loss, but the way it's currently delivered works against the people using it: electrodes held on with gel, adhesive, or tape that has to be reapplied, specific placement that eats up nursing time, and wiring that tangles with IV lines and gets in the way of fast access during an emergency. Elaya Inc. brought the team in to close that gap — a version of NMES built to sit on the patient passively and stay out of the way of everyone else working around the bed.

CT cross-sections of the same patient's leg — at ICU admission versus six months later — showing the scale of muscle loss ICUAW causes.

ICU Environment

The ICU came with fixed parameters to design around — a 20–24°C room, standard beds rated for 450 lbs, and a bacterial environment that ruled out materials that couldn't be kept clean between uses.

Since a hospital site visit wasn't possible, the team leaned on CDC anthropometric data to size the brace: femoral girth alone needed to span from 36.7 cm on a small patient to 82.6 cm on a large one, which is most of why the final design needed adjustable sizing built in rather than a fixed shape.

Design & Iteration

The problem started with two basic questions: how do you keep a device secured to a patient's leg, and how do you deliver stimulation without wiring that gets in the way of emergency access. Early sketches worked through velcro wraps, hardshell clasps, and inflatable cuffs to test those two problems directly before anything else got layered on.

From there the team deliberately widened the search. Structured brainstorming sessions, morph charts, and SCAMPER pushed past the first obvious answers, and an AI-assisted pass helped round things out — together producing 84 concepts spanning slide-on sleeves, wraps, hardshells, modular pieces, and hinged braces. Multivoting cut that list down to the strongest candidates, and a graphical decision matrix — scoring designs on fitment and usability — carried three finalists forward. A Pugh chart settled it: PneuWrap's inflatable lining gave precise pressure control but added complexity and cost; CrossWrap's hydrogel fill distributed pressure well but its rigid buckle system was harder to clean and slower to apply. FlexWrap won out for combining a large e-textile contact surface, a thin low-profile shape, and a velcro closure that was both quick to use and easy to sanitize.

01
PneuWrap — velcro-secured cotton-linen shell with an inflatable lining for adjustable pressure
02
FlexWrap (selected) — cotton-elastane wrap with layered velcro strips for even pressure and fast sizing
03
CrossWrap — linen-cotton wrap with hydrogel fill and a 4-way POM buckle system
Testing & Validation
Objective
Confirm the design meets its fitment target on a real prototype rather than just modeling it.
Method
Range-of-motion test measuring knee flexion angle from side-profile photos, with and without the brace; pressure test using a water-displacement rig positioned along the iliotibial band.
Result
Range of motion held up well — a 6.1° difference on one subject and 13.7° on the other, whose larger leg made for a tighter fit. Pressure came in even cleaner: three trials measured 1622–1626 Pa against a 2800 Pa ceiling.
Integration & Outcome

FlexWrap held up as a working prototype, not just a design on paper — secure, quick to apply, and effective at maintaining fitment without meaningfully restricting motion. What's left before it's clinic-ready is the electronics: integrating the actual e-textile and stimulation hardware into the wrap. As it stands, FlexWrap is the mechanical foundation that makes that next step possible — a way to get NMES onto ICU patients passively, without the wiring and reapplication that make the current approach so impractical.
